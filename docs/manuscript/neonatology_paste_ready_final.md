**Paste Into Methods**

Question-level taxonomy annotation was performed on the examination items and reference answer key before model-performance analysis, without using model-specific correctness outcomes to assign taxonomy labels. Reasoning complexity was operationalized a priori as `Low` when an item could be answered by direct factual retrieval or a single-step mapping from stem to answer, `Moderate` when it required one inferential step or limited integration of multiple clues, and `High` when it required multistep synthesis across diagnosis, physiology, risk stratification, or management options. Sensitivity analyses repeated the primary GEE specification under independence and unstructured working-correlation assumptions. Because category-specific cognitive-process contrasts were secondary subgroup analyses, Holm correction was applied across the four non-reference cognitive-process coefficients within each reduced model.

**Paste Into Results**

Across clustered analyses, reasoning complexity was the most consistent predictor of LLM failure. In the primary clustered GEE model including cognitive process, reasoning complexity, and universal task category, reasoning complexity was independently associated with failure (Wald chi-square=12.60, df=2, p=0.0018), whereas cognitive process (Wald chi-square=6.19, df=4, p=0.185) and universal task category (Wald chi-square=0.61, df=3, p=0.893) were not. Relative to low-complexity items, moderate-complexity items had 5.46-fold higher odds of failure (95% CI 2.12-14.08; p<0.001), and high-complexity items had 7.59-fold higher odds of failure (95% CI 1.77-32.55; p=0.006). In model-based marginal predictions from the primary clustered GEE model, the estimated probability of failure increased from 4.8% for low-complexity items to 20.8% for moderate-complexity items and 26.3% for high-complexity items, corresponding to absolute increases of 16.0 and 21.5 percentage points, respectively. The primary model showed acceptable fit (QIC 1943.66; QICu 1943.65).

Sensitivity analyses using alternative working-correlation structures did not change the main inference under the independence structure, which yielded identical estimates to the exchangeable model. Under the unstructured specification, model fit worsened (QIC 1996.70; QICu 1999.58), but reasoning complexity remained significant at the domain level (Wald chi-square=9.32, df=2, p=0.009). In that model, the moderate-complexity contrast remained significant (aOR 3.24, 95% CI 1.51-6.98; p=0.003), whereas the high-complexity contrast was attenuated and no longer individually significant (aOR 2.53, 95% CI 0.82-7.75; p=0.105).

In a supportive simplified clustered GEE model including only cognitive process and reasoning complexity, the association with reasoning complexity remained robust (Wald chi-square=13.17, df=2, p=0.001). Moderate-complexity items had 5.14-fold higher odds of failure (95% CI 2.11-12.54; p<0.001), and high-complexity items had 6.54-fold higher odds of failure (95% CI 1.57-27.25; p=0.010). In this simplified model, the Apply category was associated with lower odds of failure than Remember (aOR 0.20, 95% CI 0.06-0.69; raw p=0.011), although the overall cognitive process domain remained non-significant at the joint-test level (Wald chi-square=8.74, df=4, p=0.068). This Apply contrast remained significant after Holm correction in the simplified model (adjusted p=0.044); by contrast, the corresponding Apply contrast in the primary model was not significant after multiplicity adjustment (raw p=0.030; Holm-adjusted p=0.122). Model fit was similar to that of the primary model (QIC 1943.74; QICu 1944.98).

The expanded full-taxonomy clustered GEE model showed poorer fit (QIC 1946.44; QICu 1953.56) and unstable coefficients, supporting the use of the reduced clustered model for primary inference. In that exploratory model, reasoning complexity remained significant at the domain level (Wald chi-square=10.47, df=2, p=0.005), whereas clinical risk (Wald chi-square=0.56, df=3, p=0.905), difficulty (Wald chi-square=0.39, df=2, p=0.821), and universal task category (Wald chi-square=0.43, df=3, p=0.934) were not. Reference recoding of the cognitive-process variable left the quasi-likelihood and QICu unchanged, but the QIC trace penalty differed slightly in this unstable expanded model; therefore, the reference-recoded fit was treated only as a diagnostic rather than as a separate candidate model.

**Paste Into Discussion**

These findings suggest that, within this neonatal examination dataset, reasoning burden was more informative than broad topic labels for characterizing where LLMs fail. Questions requiring moderate or high reasoning complexity were consistently associated with higher error odds than low-complexity questions, and model-based marginal predictions showed a marked increase in failure probability across complexity strata.

This distinction matters because neonatal decision-making rarely depends on factual recall alone. Instead, it often requires layered synthesis of physiology, diagnosis, risk assessment, and management in time-sensitive settings. At the same time, these examination-based findings should not be interpreted as a direct estimate of bedside deployment safety; rather, they identify a plausible vulnerability that merits further prospective testing in clinically realistic settings.

The role of cognitive process was more nuanced. Although the simplified model suggested lower failure odds for Apply relative to Remember and this subgroup contrast remained significant after Holm correction, the full cognitive-process domain was not significant across clustered specifications and the corresponding Apply contrast was not robust in the primary model after multiplicity adjustment. We therefore interpret cognitive process as a secondary and model-dependent finding rather than a primary independent determinant of failure.

The non-significant clinical-risk domain in the exploratory full-taxonomy model also warrants caution. A null association for the risk label does not imply that errors on clinically consequential neonatal questions are unimportant or that high-risk use cases are safe. Rather, it suggests that this coarse item-level risk categorization did not add explanatory value once overlapping taxonomy features were included, and that the present sample may not have been powered to detect finer safety gradients. Similarly, expanded taxonomy models that simultaneously included several overlapping domains produced poorer fit and unstable coefficients, emphasizing the value of parsimonious modeling when evaluating clustered benchmark data.

**Paste Into Supplementary Methods**

Supplementary analyses were performed to assess model stability, collinearity, and robustness of inference. First, categorical overlap among taxonomy predictors was examined using verified Cramer's V matrices, and multicollinearity was further assessed using variance inflation factor diagnostics. These analyses demonstrated substantial overlap between knowledge dimension and cognitive process, with additional overlap among reasoning complexity, difficulty, and clinical risk. Accordingly, broader model specifications were treated cautiously.

Second, we compared candidate clustered GEE specifications using QIC and QICu. Among reduced clustered models, specifications that excluded highly overlapping domains showed better fit than the expanded full-taxonomy model. The primary model including cognitive process, reasoning complexity, and universal task category was selected for main inference because it retained clinical interpretability while avoiding the instability observed in broader specifications. A simplified clustered model including only cognitive process and reasoning complexity was also examined as a supportive analysis. Reference-coding diagnostics were reported separately: quasi-likelihood and QICu were invariant to cognitive-process reference recoding, whereas QIC was not interpreted for the recoded unstable expanded model because its trace penalty changed under reparameterization.

Third, we calculated model-based marginal predicted probabilities from the primary clustered GEE model to quantify the absolute change in failure risk across reasoning-complexity strata. This analysis showed that the predicted probability of failure increased by 16.0 percentage points for moderate-complexity items and by 21.5 percentage points for high-complexity items relative to low-complexity items. Finally, we fit a clustered linear probability model using the same reduced taxonomy structure to assess whether the direction of associations was preserved on the absolute probability scale. An expanded clustered GEE model including knowledge dimension, cognitive process, reasoning complexity, clinical risk, difficulty, and universal task category was retained as an exploratory supplementary analysis, but not used for primary inference because of poorer fit and unstable coefficients.
